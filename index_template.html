Established at the end of 1967

One of the first masjids in Bolton.

The name Taiyabah — from the Arabic Tayyab, meaning purity — was given by the late Hazrat Sheikh Moulana Mohammed Zakariyya Saheb, author of Fazaail-e-Amaal.

01

History

The masjid was originally located in Canning Street, where Moulana Faruq Desai was appointed as its first Imam.

In February 1988, to accommodate the growing community, the present building on Blackburn Road was purchased — formerly a Congregational Sunday School. The first Imam in the new masjid was Qari Yakub Nanjee Saheb, followed by Qari Farook Saheb.

What Is Islam?

A plain introduction to the faith, its core beliefs, and what it means to be Muslim.

The name of the religion is Islam, an Arabic word built from a root meaning “peace” and “submission.” Its teaching is simple to state, if not always simple to live: a person finds real peace by submitting to Almighty God — Allah — in heart, soul and deed. The same root gives us “Assalamu alaykum” (“Peace be with you”), the greeting Muslims exchange the world over.

Who is a Muslim?

Someone who believes in and consciously follows Islam is called a Muslim — from the same Arabic root as “Islam” itself. In short: the religion is Islam, and its followers are Muslims.

How many, and where?

Islam is one of the world's major religions, with well over a billion followers — roughly a fifth of the world's population. Alongside Judaism and Christianity, it is one of the three great Abrahamic, monotheistic faiths. Although Islam is often associated with the Arab world, fewer than one in ten Muslims worldwide are Arab; Muslims live in almost every country, across every nation, colour and race. Most belong to one of two main traditions, Sunni and Shia, which share the same core beliefs set out below.

Who is Allah?

Allah is the proper name for Almighty God, often translated simply as “God.” Allah is also known by names that describe His attributes — the Creator, the Sustainer, the Merciful, the Compassionate, among many others. Because Muslims believe Allah alone is the Creator, He alone is deserving of worship and devotion. Islam holds firmly to monotheism: directing worship or prayer toward saints, prophets, other people, or nature is considered a serious wrong.

What Muslims believe

Muslim belief rests on six foundations, known as the Articles of Faith:

  • Faith in the oneness of God
  • Faith in the angels
  • Faith in the prophets
  • Faith in the revealed books, including the Qur'an
  • Faith in the afterlife
  • Faith in divine decree

Muslims believe the Qur'an was revealed to the Prophet Muhammad ﷲ over some 23 years, and that he was the last in a line of prophets that includes Ibrahim, Musa and ʿIsa (Abraham, Moses and Jesus, peace be upon them all).

The Five Pillars of Islam

In Islam, faith and action go together — belief in Allah calls for obedience to Him. Muslims consider everything done according to Allah's guidance to be an act of worship, but five formal acts anchor a Muslim's practice. These are the Five Pillars:

1

Shahaadah · Declaration of Faith

The foundation of a Muslim's spiritual life: bearing witness that “there is no god but Allah, and Muhammad ﷲ is the messenger of Allah.”

2

Salat · Prayer

Muslims pray five times daily, at times set by the sun's movement: near dawn (Fajr), after noon (Zuhr), in the afternoon (Asr), after sunset (Maghrib) and at nightfall (Isha).

3

Zakat · Almsgiving

Muslims give a set share of their savings each year to those in need — a duty, not simply a kindness.

4

Sawm · Fasting in Ramadhan

Once a year, Muslims fast from dawn to sunset for the month of Ramadhan, abstaining from food, drink and other worldly needs.

5

Hajj · Pilgrimage

Every able-bodied Muslim who can afford to must perform the pilgrimage to Makkah at least once in their lifetime — the largest annual gathering of people on earth, and a demonstration of the unity of Muslims before the one God.

The two Eid festivals mark key moments in this calendar — Eid al-Fitr at the end of Ramadhan, and Eid al-Adha during the Hajj season — both occasions of communal prayer, charity and celebration.

Daily life as a Muslim

Muslims do not live in disregard of God, nor do they withdraw from the world to devote themselves only to worship. Islam calls for a balance: fulfilling worldly responsibilities and enjoying this life, while staying mindful of one's duties to Allah and to others. That mindfulness touches morals and manners, business ethics, modesty in dress and behaviour, dietary rules, marriage and family life, care for children and the elderly, and standing against racism and prejudice — as well as how Muslims relate to people of other faiths and none.

Want to know more, or have a question? The masjid's imams are available for advice, and everyone is welcome to visit for one of the five daily prayers.

The Five Pillars of Islam

The five acts every Muslim builds their life around — what each one asks, and who it applies to.

Islam sets out five acts that every Muslim is expected to build their life around: the declaration of faith, the five daily prayers, the annual charity payment, the fast of Ramadhan, and the pilgrimage to Makkah. They are called the Five Pillars because they hold the structure up. Belief comes first — but Islam is a religion of practice as well as conviction, and these five are where the practice starts.

1 · Shahaadah — the declaration of faith

The Shahaadah is a single sentence: “I bear witness that there is no god but Allah, and I bear witness that Muhammad ﷲ is the Messenger of Allah.” Said sincerely, with understanding of what it means, it is what makes a person a Muslim — there is no other ceremony required.

It is not a phrase said once and then filed away. It sits inside the call to prayer, inside every one of the five daily prayers, and is what a Muslim hopes to have on their lips at the end of their life. The first half rejects the worship of anything besides God; the second accepts that the way to worship Him was taught by His final messenger.

2 · Salaah — the five daily prayers

Muslims pray five times a day at times set by the movement of the sun, not the clock: Fajr near dawn, Zuhr after midday, Asr in the afternoon, Maghrib just after sunset and Isha at nightfall. Because the times follow the sun, they shift a little every day and a great deal across a British year — which is why the masjid publishes a timetable rather than a fixed set of times.

Prayer is preceded by wudu, a short ritual washing, and performed facing the Ka'bah in Makkah. It can be prayed almost anywhere that is clean, but praying in congregation at the masjid carries far greater reward, and the Friday midday prayer — Jumu'ah, which replaces Zuhr and includes a sermon — is a congregational obligation for men.

Prayer becomes obligatory at puberty, and Islam makes room for human circumstance: someone who cannot stand may sit, someone who cannot sit may pray lying down, and travellers shorten certain prayers. What is not permitted is simply letting them go.

3 · Zakaat — the annual charity payment

Zakaat is not the same as voluntary charity. Voluntary giving is called sadaqah, and a Muslim may give as much of it as they wish. Zakaat is a due — a fixed obligation on wealth, paid once a lunar year.

The rate is 2.5% of qualifying wealth: savings, gold and silver, and business stock, among other categories. It is only owed if that wealth stays above a minimum threshold, the nisaab, for a full lunar year. Below the nisaab, nothing is owed at all — Zakaat is a duty on those with a surplus, never a burden on those without one.

The Qur'an names who it may go to, including the poor, the destitute, those in debt, and the traveller who has run out of means. The framing matters: Zakaat is treated as the recipient's right, not the giver's generosity.

4 · Sawm — fasting in Ramadhan

Once a year, for the whole month of Ramadhan, Muslims fast from first light until sunset — no food, no drink, no smoking — and use the month for extra prayer, extra Qur'an and extra charity. The intention is self-restraint rather than deprivation: holding the tongue and the temper as much as the appetite.

Fasting is obligatory on adult Muslims in good health, and Islam exempts those it would harm — the ill, the pregnant, the elderly, travellers, and children before puberty.

Read: What Is Ramadhan?

5 · Hajj — the pilgrimage to Makkah

Every Muslim who is physically and financially able must perform the pilgrimage to Makkah once in their lifetime. It takes place over five days in Dhul Hijjah, the last month of the Islamic year, and is the largest annual gathering of people anywhere on earth.

“Able” is a genuine condition, not a formality: a person must be well enough to make the journey and able to pay for it from surplus wealth, without borrowing and without leaving dependants short. Someone who never becomes able is not blamed for never going.

Read: What Is Hajj?

Why “pillars”?

The image is deliberate. A pillar carries weight; remove one and the structure sags. But pillars are the frame of a building, not the building itself. Islam treats honest dealing in business, care for parents, kindness to neighbours, keeping your word and controlling your temper as acts of worship too. The Five Pillars are what a Muslim's practice is built on — not the sum total of what is asked.

New to any of this, or returning to it after a while? Nobody at the masjid expects you to arrive knowing it all. The imams are available for advice, and everyone is welcome at any of the five daily prayers.

What Is Ramadhan?

The month of fasting — what it involves, who it applies to, and what happens at the masjid.

Ramadhan is the ninth month of the Islamic calendar and the month in which Muslims fast. For twenty-nine or thirty days, from first light until sunset, adult Muslims go without food and drink — then break the fast together at sunset. Fasting in Ramadhan is the fourth of the Five Pillars of Islam, and for most Muslim families it is the most anticipated month of the year.

When is Ramadhan?

The Islamic calendar follows the moon rather than the sun, so a lunar year runs about eleven days shorter than the Gregorian one. Ramadhan therefore moves earlier each year, working its way through every season over roughly thirty-three years. That is why a Ramadhan in Britain can mean a nineteen-hour fast in midsummer or a comfortable eleven-hour one in midwinter.

The month begins with the sighting of the new moon, so the exact start is confirmed only shortly beforehand. The masjid announces the first fast, the Ramadhan timetable and the nightly Taraweeh arrangements ahead of the month.

What fasting actually involves

From the start of Fajr — first light, before sunrise — until Maghrib at sunset, a fasting person takes nothing by mouth: no food, no water, no smoking. The pre-dawn meal is called suhoor; the meal that breaks the fast at sunset is iftar, traditionally opened with dates and water, following the practice of the Prophet ﷲ.

Fasting is not only about the stomach. The point of it is self-restraint, and a fast is diminished by a day spent arguing, backbiting or losing your temper. The Prophet ﷲ warned that whoever does not give up false speech and acting upon it, God has no need of him giving up his food and drink.

Who fasts — and who doesn't

Fasting is obligatory on adult Muslims of sound mind and reasonable health. Islam does not ask anyone to fast at the cost of their wellbeing, and the following are excused:

  • Children before puberty — though many fast part-days to build the habit
  • Anyone unwell, or whose illness fasting would worsen
  • Travellers on a long journey
  • Women who are pregnant or breastfeeding, where fasting would harm them or the child
  • Women during menstruation and post-natal bleeding
  • The elderly and the chronically ill, for whom fasting is not realistic

Days missed for a temporary reason are made up later in the year. Where fasting will never again be possible — old age, or a permanent condition — a payment called fidyah feeds a person in need for each missed day instead.

If you manage a health condition, take regular medication, or are living with diabetes, have both conversations before Ramadhan: one with your GP or pharmacist, and one with an imam. Neither replaces the other, and both are better had in advance than in the middle of the month.

More than going without food

Ramadhan is a month of worship, not just of abstinence. Muslims read more Qur'an — many complete the whole book across the month — give more in charity, and pray Taraweeh, a long congregational night prayer held at the masjid after Isha. In the last ten nights, some observe i'tikaaf, withdrawing into the masjid for a period of concentrated worship.

Laylatul Qadr — the Night of Decree

Somewhere in the last ten nights, most often sought on the odd-numbered ones, falls Laylatul Qadr — the night the Qur'an describes as better than a thousand months. Its exact date is not disclosed, and that is part of the point: the search keeps worshippers alert right through the final stretch of the month rather than saving their effort for one known night.

Zakaat al-Fitr and Eid

Before the Eid prayer, each household pays Zakaat al-Fitr — a small fixed amount for every member of the household, children included — so that nobody is left out of the celebration. The masjid announces the amount each year.

Ramadhan then closes with Eid al-Fitr: the Eid prayer in congregation, new clothes, visits to family, and food in the daytime after a month of going without.

Ramadhan at the masjid

Through Ramadhan the masjid keeps its five daily prayers and adds Taraweeh each night. The month's prayer timetable, the Zakaat al-Fitr amount and the Eid prayer arrangements are announced in advance and posted here and on the masjid's social channels.

Everyone is welcome, whether you have fasted every Ramadhan of your life or are attempting your first.

Fasting for the first time, or working out how to manage it alongside a health condition or a demanding job? The imams are happy to talk it through, and prayer times for every day of the year are on this site.

What Is Hajj?

The pilgrimage to Makkah — who it is required of, what pilgrims do, and how Umrah differs.

Hajj is the pilgrimage to Makkah, and the fifth of the Five Pillars of Islam. Every Muslim who is physically and financially able is required to perform it once in their lifetime. It takes place over five days in Dhul Hijjah, the twelfth and final month of the Islamic calendar, and brings well over a million people from every country on earth into the same few square miles at the same time.

Who has to go?

Not everyone. Hajj becomes obligatory only on a Muslim who is adult, of sound mind, and able — istitaa'ah in Arabic. Able means genuinely able: well enough to make a physically demanding journey, and able to pay for it out of surplus wealth — without borrowing, without leaving debts unpaid, and without leaving dependants at home short of what they need.

Many Muslims save for years or decades. Some never become able, and Islam does not hold that against them. It is worth saying plainly, because families sometimes feel a pressure that the religion itself does not place on them.

Ihraam — leaving your status at the border

Before entering the sacred precincts around Makkah, pilgrims enter a state called ihraam. Men wear two unstitched white cloths; women wear ordinary modest clothing with the face and hands uncovered. While in ihraam a pilgrim does not use perfume, cut hair or nails, hunt, engage in marital relations, or argue.

The visual effect is the point. Wealth, class, nationality and standing all disappear into the same two white sheets. Everybody arrives at Arafah dressed identically, and dressed much as they will be when they are buried.

What pilgrims actually do

1

Tawaaf

Circling the Ka'bah seven times. The Ka'bah is not worshipped — it is the direction Muslims face in prayer, the focal point the whole community turns around.

2

Sa'i

Walking seven times between the hills of Safa and Marwa, retracing Haajar's search for water for her infant son Ismaa'il.

3

Arafah

On the ninth of Dhul Hijjah, standing on the plain of Arafah from midday until sunset in prayer and supplication. Miss Arafah and there is no Hajj — it is the one part that cannot be substituted.

4

Muzdalifah & the Jamaraat

A night spent under open sky at Muzdalifah, then the stoning of three pillars at Mina over the following days — a physical enactment of rejecting temptation.

5

Qurbani & release from ihraam

An animal is sacrificed and the meat distributed to those in need. Men then shave or trim their hair and women trim a small amount, and the restrictions of ihraam are lifted.

Pilgrims return to the Ka'bah for Tawaaf al-Ifaadah, and most conclude with a farewell tawaaf before leaving Makkah. Many also travel on to Madinah to visit the Prophet's ﷲ mosque — a much-loved journey, though not part of Hajj itself.

Umrah is not Hajj

Umrah — sometimes called the lesser pilgrimage — can be performed at any time of year. It involves tawaaf and sa'i, but not Arafah, Muzdalifah or the Jamaraat, and takes hours rather than days. It is greatly rewarded and many Muslims perform it more than once, but it does not discharge the obligation of Hajj.

Eid al-Adha and Qurbani

While the pilgrims are at Mina, Muslims everywhere else mark Eid al-Adha, the festival of sacrifice, commemorating Ibrahim's willingness to sacrifice his son at God's command. It falls on the tenth of Dhul Hijjah and runs for three days. Those who can afford it arrange a Qurbani, with the meat shared between family, neighbours and those in need.

Thinking of going?

Hajj is a serious undertaking — a visa, a licensed tour operator, vaccinations, and real physical preparation for long days of walking in heat. Choosing an operator badly is one of the most common and costly mistakes British pilgrims make, so take advice before you pay anyone.

Speak to someone who has been recently, and speak to one of the masjid's imams. Both will tell you things a brochure will not.

The masjid shop will stock ihraam, prayer accessories and the practical bits pilgrims always end up needing. It isn't taking orders yet — but you can see what's coming.

Ghusl Workshop

How to wash and prepare a body for burial — a duty that falls on the community, and one most people have never been shown.

Format
One session
Places
15
If you need help right nowFuneral Services › Masjid office · 5pm–7pm01204 535 997 ›

Washing the body of someone who has died is a fard kifayah — an obligation on the community as a whole rather than on any one person. As long as enough people can do it, the duty is discharged for everyone. Where nobody can, it falls on everyone.

In practice most families discover, in the worst week of their lives, that they do not know how and do not know who to ask. This workshop exists so that fewer families are in that position — and so that when it happens on your street, someone nearby can step in.

What the workshop covers

  • What happens in the hours after a death, and who to ring first
  • What is needed, and where the masjid keeps it
  • The washing itself, taken through step by step
  • Shrouding — the kafan, and how it is prepared
  • The adab of it: modesty, gentleness, and holding your tongue afterwards
  • What follows — janāzah prayer, and the burial

It is taught at the masjid, which has its own mortuary, so what you are shown is what you would actually be doing in the room.

Practical details

Who for
Adults aged 16 and over. Men's and women's sessions are run separately, because men wash men and women wash women.
When
A one-off session, run periodically rather than to a fixed timetable. Register and the office will tell you when the next one is.
Places
Fifteen per session. After that, names go on the list for the session after.
Experience
None expected. Most people who come have never done it. Those who have are welcome too — the masjid needs more of you.

الوَصِيَّة

The Importance of an Islamic Will

مَا حَقُّ امْرِئٍ مُسْلِمٍ لَهُ شَيْءٌ يُوصِي فِيهِ يَبِيتُ لَيْلَتَيْنِ إِلَّا وَوَصِيَّتُهُ مَكْتُوبَةٌ عِنْدَهُ

"A Muslim who has something to leave behind should not let two nights pass without having his will written and kept ready."

The Prophet ﷺ · Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī & Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim

كُتِبَ عَلَيْكُمْ إِذَا حَضَرَ أَحَدَكُمُ الْمَوْتُ إِنْ تَرَكَ خَيْرًا الْوَصِيَّةُ

"When death approaches one of you, and you leave behind good, it is prescribed that you make a bequest — a duty upon those mindful of Allah."

Qur'an · Sūrah al-Baqarah, 2:180

Why it matters

When a Muslim passes away without having made their wishes clear, their estate is left to be settled according to the default rules of the country they lived in — which may not reflect Islamic guidance on how a person's wealth should be distributed. An Islamic will sets out, in your own words, while you are able to:

  • Who is entitled to benefit from your estate, and in what shares
  • How your property and other assets should be dealt with
  • Who you trust to carry this out on your behalf — your executors
  • Any funeral wishes you would like to be followed

Distributing your estate

Islamic inheritance follows fixed shares (Farā'iḍ) set out in the Qur'an for specific family members. A will does not override these shares, but it lets you address anything the fixed shares do not cover — for example, up to a third of your estate may be left to people or causes outside your Islamic heirs, such as a charitable gift. Because these calculations depend on your own family circumstances, they are best worked through individually with the imam or a solicitor experienced in Islamic wills.

General information, not advice

This is a starting point, not legal, financial or religious advice for your own circumstances. UK inheritance tax and probate rules are detailed and change over time — please speak to a qualified solicitor to write your will, and to the imam for guidance on the Islamic shares that apply to your family.

Privacy notice

How Taiyabah Masjid handles information collected through this website.

This notice covers this website only. It explains what the website collects, why, and what you can ask us to do about it. It is written to be read, not to be survived.

The short version. The only thing this website collects is a hall hire request, and only if you choose to send one. There are no cookies, no analytics, no advertising, and no tracking of any kind. Almost everything on the site is served from the site itself; the two exceptions are named below.

Who is responsible

Bolton Central Islamic Society, a registered charity (number 1041569), of 31a Draycott Street, Bolton BL1 8HD, operating as Taiyabah Masjid. We are the data controller for this website. You can reach us on 01204 535 997 or at info@taiyabahmasjid.com.

What this website collects

One thing, and only when you send it: a request to hire a hall. If you complete the hall hire form we receive your first name, surname, address, telephone number, and the date, time slot and hall you asked about.

The form also asks whether you are a member of the masjid, because the answer decides which rate you are quoted. That answer is not sent to us and is not stored anywhere. It is used to work out the figure shown on your screen and is then discarded when you leave the page. The figure itself is not stored either. Whether or not you are a member of a mosque says something about your religion, and we would rather not hold that than hold it carefully.

If you do not send a booking request, this website collects nothing about you at all.

Why we need it, and what allows us to hold it

We need your name and telephone number to call you back, and your address because a hall hire is a contract and we need to know who we are contracting with. Our lawful basis is taking steps at your request before entering into a contract (UK GDPR Article 6(1)(b)).

We do not use your details for anything else. We will not add you to a mailing list, and we will not pass them to anyone else for marketing.

Who can see it

Booking requests are visible to masjid office staff who administer hall bookings, and to nobody else. The website itself cannot read them back — it can only add new ones.

The requests are stored in a database hosted by Supabase, on servers in London, United Kingdom. Supabase acts as our processor: they hold the data on our behalf and do not use it for their own purposes.

So that a request doesn't sit unnoticed, an alert is emailed to the office when one arrives. That email is sent for us by Resend, an email delivery service acting as our processor. It contains your name, your telephone number and the date, slot and hall you asked about. Your address is deliberately left out of it and stays in the booking system, so that it isn't copied into inboxes or handled by the email provider at all.

How long we keep it

Booking requests are deleted six months after the date you asked about. Requests that were declined or cancelled are deleted after three months. We keep them that long so that we can deal with any question about a booking after the event, and no longer.

Cookies and tracking

This website sets no cookies. It does not use Google Analytics or any other analytics service. It does not carry advertising and does not track you between sites.

Almost everything the site needs — its typefaces, its own photographs and its code — is served from the site itself, so on nearly every page, simply visiting tells no other company that you were here. There are two exceptions, and they are set out below rather than buried.

The first is the hall hire page. Opening it asks our booking database which dates are already taken, so that the calendar can show you something true. That request carries no information about you — it asks only which sessions are spoken for — and it is not made on any other page. The database is ours, hosted for us in London.

The second is the media page. Each video card shows the still image YouTube holds for that video, and those images come from Google's servers rather than ours. That means that when you open the media page, Google can see your IP address and roughly where in the world you are, before you have clicked on anything. We ask the browser not to tell Google which page you came from, and the images are only requested as you scroll down to them, but we cannot stop the request itself. This happens on the media page and nowhere else. If you would rather it did not happen at all, do not open that page.

Other links on the site go out to other companies, and they only do anything if you choose to click them: Open in Maps on the contact page opens Google Maps, the video cards on the media page open YouTube, and our WhatsApp group link opens WhatsApp. Once you follow one of those links you are on their website and their own privacy policy applies. We deliberately do not embed a map, a video player or a chat widget anywhere in the page — the only thing that loads from elsewhere is the video still images described above.
